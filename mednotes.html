🤰 Clinical Pharmacology in Pregnancy

🛡️ 1. Core Prescribing Rules
  • Use the fewest drugs possible and the lowest effective dose.
  • Prefer drugs with long safety experience in pregnancy.
  • Check gestational age — risk profile changes by trimester.
  • Prefer monotherapy when possible.
  • Reassess maternal benefit vs fetal risk at every decision.
  • Document counseling and shared decision-making.

⚠ Yellow = Use with caution
✕ Red = Contraindicated / avoid
When to seek specialist help: known teratogen exposure · polypharmacy · epilepsy · anticoagulation · psychiatric disease · transplant medicine · severe hypertension · maternal organ dysfunction · fetal growth concerns.

💡 4. High-Yield Practical Pearls
  • Organogenesis risk is highest in the 1st trimester.
  • NSAIDs become more concerning after 20 weeks and especially late pregnancy.
  • ACE inhibitors/ARBs can injure fetal kidneys.
  • Warfarin causes fetal warfarin syndrome — heparins are preferred.
  • Retinoids and methotrexate are classic major teratogens.
  • Near delivery: consider neonatal respiratory, bleeding, withdrawal, adaptation risks.

🧬 9. Teratology & Critical Periods of Development

📖 Overview

Teratology = study of congenital malformations and developmental toxicity caused by drugs, chemicals, infections, radiation, or maternal disease. Harm depends mainly on timing, dose, duration, placental transfer, and fetal susceptibility. Risk may involve structural defects, pregnancy loss, growth restriction, or later functional impairment.

📝 Principles of Teratogenesis
  1. Susceptibility depends on fetal genotype + maternal factors.
  2. Dose & duration matter: higher exposure usually ↑ risk.
  3. Timing is critical: same drug can be harmless in one stage, dangerous in another.
  4. Placental transfer depends on lipophilicity, size, protein binding, transporters.
  5. Teratogens produce characteristic patterns of malformation, not random injury.
  6. Outcomes: death, malformation, growth restriction, functional/neurobehavioral toxicity.
🕐 Critical Periods of Development
  • 0–2 weeks (Pre-implantation): All-or-none effect — embryonic death or normal development.
  • 3–8 weeks (Organogenesis): Highest risk of major structural malformations.
  • 9 weeks–birth (Fetal period): Growth, functional maturation, CNS and endocrine vulnerability.

📄 FDA PLLR — Since June 30, 2015

Replaces letter categories with narrative, evidence-based labeling in 3 sections:

  • A. Pregnancy: Risk Summary, Clinical Considerations, Data. No overall risk category.
  • B. Lactation: Drug excretion in human milk; effects on infant & milk production.
  • C. Reproductive Potential: Effects on fertility, pregnancy testing, contraception (females & males).

🔄 11. Why Doses Change in Pregnancy

Goal: Maintain maternal efficacy while minimizing fetal/neonatal risk. Dose by clinical response, therapeutic targets, and drug levels when available.

Physiologic changes: ↑ Plasma volume & total body water (↓ concentration of hydrophilic drugs) · ↓ Albumin (↑ free fraction of highly bound drugs) · ↑ Renal blood flow & GFR (faster renal clearance) · Altered hepatic enzyme activity · Net effect: concentrations fall in pregnancy, rebound postpartum.

Greatest changes in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters.

🧠 Lamotrigine

  • Clearance ↑ → levels fall.
  • Underdosing → breakthrough seizures.
  • Trough q ~4 weeks.
  • Increase stepwise (~20–25% at a time).
  • Postpartum: reduce to pre-pregnancy dose; recheck level in 1–2 weeks.
  • Watch for postpartum toxicity: dizziness, diplopia, ataxia, nausea.

💊 Methadone

  • Metabolism ↑; duration shortens.
  • May need dose ↑, esp. late pregnancy.
  • Split dosing may help.
  • Adjust by symptoms: craving, withdrawal, oversedation.
  • Do not undertreat OUD.
  • Postpartum: no automatic large reduction; reassess.

🦠 Beta-lactams

  • Penicillins, cephalosporins.
  • ↑ Vd + renal elimination → lower exposure.
  • Severe infection: higher dose, shorter interval, extended infusion.
  • Return to standard adult dosing after delivery.

📊 Therapeutic Drug Monitoring (TDM)

  • Best for narrow therapeutic index drugs.
  • Baseline target level before pregnancy.
  • Repeat at planned intervals & postpartum.
  • Free levels may be more informative when binding changes.
  • Examples: Lamotrigine, lithium, some AEDs, digoxin, immunosuppressants.
⚠️ Rule: Increase cautiously during pregnancy — de-escalate thoughtfully after birth. Review all pregnancy-adjusted meds within 24–72 hrs of delivery.

🔬 12. Foundations of Pharmacology — PK/ADME in Pregnancy

Key principle: Maternal physiologic changes begin early and progress throughout pregnancy. They affect all ADME processes and drug response. Consider gestational age at every prescribing decision.

🍽️ A — Absorption
  • Nausea/vomiting, delayed gastric emptying, ↑ gastric pH, ↓ motility, ↑ splanchnic flow.
  • Weak bases: absorption ↑; weak acids: absorption ↓.
  • Examples: ↑ antacids, metformin; ↓ aspirin, furosemide.
  • Prefer reliable formulations; consider non-oral routes if severe N/V.
💧 D — Distribution
  • ↑ Total body water, fat, lean mass.
  • ↑ Plasma volume 40–50%.
  • ↓ Albumin 20–30% & α1-acid glycoprotein.
  • ↑ Vd for hydrophilic (aminoglycosides, lithium) and lipophilic drugs.
  • ↑ Free fraction of highly bound drugs (phenytoin, warfarin, valproate).
  • May need higher loading/maintenance doses; monitor free (unbound) levels.

⚙️ 13. Pharmacodynamics in Pregnancy

PD = what the drug does to the body. Pregnancy can change tissue responsiveness even when drug concentration is unchanged. Changes are drug-, organ-, and trimester-specific, and less predictable than PK changes.

🔧 Physiologic changes affecting PD
  • Hormonal environment: E2, progesterone, hCG, prolactin alter set points.
  • Cardiovascular adaptation: ↑ CO and vasodilation change response to vasoactive drugs.
  • Autonomic & receptor adaptation: altered receptor density, affinity, downstream signaling.
  • Homeostatic resetting: renal, respiratory, endocrine, coagulation systems at new baselines.
🎯 Key PD mechanisms
  • Altered drug sensitivity: increased or blunted effect at same concentration; narrower therapeutic window.
  • Receptor changes: up/down-regulation, affinity changes, tissue-specific expression shifts.
  • Post-receptor signaling changes: G-protein, second messenger, NO, prostaglandin, calcium channel, adrenergic pathways.
  • Compensatory responses: baroreflexes, RAAS, insulin resistance, respiratory drive.

💡 High-yield PD pearls
  • Same concentration ≠ same effect.
  • PD changes are less predictable than PK changes.
  • Receptors, signaling pathways, compensatory responses may change.
  • Clinical monitoring is essential — response may vary.
  • Reassess postpartum as physiology returns to baseline.

🔁 14. Placental Drug Transfer

Overview: Placenta is a dynamic barrier regulating gas/nutrient/xenobiotic exchange. Drug transfer depends on physicochemical properties, placental transporters, and gestational age.

Barrier: syncytiotrophoblast → cytotrophoblast → basement membrane → fetal capillary endothelium.

Factors affecting transfer

  • Molecular weight: < 500 Da crosses more easily.
  • Lipophilicity: More lipophilic → crosses faster.
  • Ionization (pKa): Non-ionized form crosses more readily.
  • Protein binding: Only free (unbound) drug crosses.
  • Gestational age: Transfer ↑ as pregnancy advances.
  • Maternal factors: pH, CO, diseases (e.g., preeclampsia).
  • Drug transporters: Efflux transporters can limit fetal exposure.

Mechanisms

  • Passive diffusion (main): Anesthetics, opioids, benzodiazepines, most antibiotics, SSRIs.
  • Active transport: L-DOPA (LAT1), folate, some antivirals.
  • Endocytosis: Insulin, IgG, transferrin, some nanoparticles.

🚑 15. Emergency & Critical Care Pharmacology

🎯 Core principles
  • Maternal stabilization comes first — fetal benefit depends on maternal resuscitation.
  • Multidisciplinary care: OB, ICU, anesthesia, cardiology/hematology, neonatology, pharmacy.
  • PK altered: ↑ Vd, ↑ renal clearance, ↓ albumin.
  • Do not delay life-saving drugs because of pregnancy when benefits outweigh fetal risk.
  • Reassess maternal hemodynamics, oxygenation, fetal status, and need for urgent delivery.
⚡ Seizures and Eclampsia

Magnesium sulfate (first-line): Loading 4–6 g IV over 20–30 min. Maintenance 1–2 g/hour. Recurrent seizure: additional 2–4 g IV over 5 min. Continue 24 hrs after delivery or last seizure.

Supportive: Control severe HTN: IV labetalol, IV hydralazine, oral IR nifedipine. Refractory: diazepam/lorazepam rescue; consider levetiracetam or phenytoin in selected cases. Monitor reflexes, RR, urine output.

Antidote: Calcium gluconate 1 g IV slowly for toxicity (loss of patellar reflexes, respiratory depression).

🫁 Thromboembolism (DVT/PE)

Preferred anticoagulant: LMWH (enoxaparin, dalteparin). LMWH does not cross placenta.

Start therapeutic LMWH when acute VTE strongly suspected, unless contraindicated, while testing proceeds.

Use UFH when rapid reversal needed, around delivery, severe renal impairment, or hemodynamic instability.

Massive PE: IV UFH initially; consider thrombolysis (alteplase) for life-threatening PE.

Avoid: DOACs; Warfarin in pregnancy (can be used postpartum, compatible with breastfeeding).

💔 Cardiac Arrest

Standard ACLS meds & defibrillation — pregnancy does not change shock dose.

  • Epinephrine 1 mg IV q3–5 min.
  • Refractory VF/pulseless VT: amiodarone per ACLS.
  • Continuous left uterine displacement when fundal height ≥ umbilicus.
  • IV access above diaphragm; early airway management.
  • If on IV magnesium & toxicity suspected → stop magnesium, give calcium.
  • Prepare resuscitative delivery if no ROSC by 5 min.
  • Think: B.H.E.D.S. + H's & T's (Bleeding, Hypertension/Preeclampsia, Embolism, Drugs/Anesthesia, Fever/Sepsis).
🦠 Sepsis and Septic Shock

Vasopressor: Norepinephrine first; add vasopressin if needed; consider epinephrine/dopamine in selected shock states.

Antibiotics: Broad-spectrum within 1 hr (pip-tazo, cefepime, meropenem; add vancomycin for MRSA).

Fluids: 1–2 L balanced crystalloids early (first 3 hrs).

Steroid: Consider IV hydrocortisone in refractory shock.

Source control is essential — if uterine source, delivery/evacuation may be needed.

🚭 16. Smoking, Alcohol, Opioid & Illicit Drug Use

🚫 Smoking Cessation
  • Behavioral (first-line): counseling, CBT, digital support, partner involvement, quit date + follow-up.
  • NRT (patch, gum, lozenge, inhaler, spray): Generally safe (2nd tri); use lowest effective dose; short-acting forms if needed; start before quit date.
  • Bupropion SR: Use if benefits outweigh risks; monitor mood; avoid in eating disorders or seizure history.
  • Varenicline: Not recommended (limited data).
  • Benefits of cessation: ↓ miscarriage, preterm birth, IUGR; ↑ placental function & O₂ delivery; ↓ SIDS & childhood respiratory disease.
🍷 Alcohol Use Disorder
  • No safe level of alcohol in pregnancy.
  • Screen at every visit; brief intervention + referrals.
  • Withdrawal: Benzodiazepines (diazepam, lorazepam) preferred; thiamine 100 mg IV/IM daily before dextrose.
  • Avoid: Disulfiram, topiramate, naltrexone.
  • Fetal: FASD (facial anomalies, growth restriction, CNS dysfunction); ↑ miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm delivery.
💊 Opioid Use Disorder (MOUD)
  • Methadone: Full agonist; safe & effective; reduces illicit use & obstetric complications.
  • Buprenorphine: Partial agonist; safe & effective; lower NAS severity than methadone.
  • Naltrexone: Not recommended (requires detox before initiation).
  • Do NOT abruptly withdraw — ↑ relapse, overdose, preterm labor, fetal distress.
  • Breastfeeding encouraged if stable on MOUD & no illicit use.

❤️ 17. Cardiovascular Diseases in Pregnancy

🎯 Core principles
  • Mother first, fetus protected whenever possible.
  • Use drugs with pregnancy safety experience; avoid teratogenic cardiovascular drugs.
  • Multidisciplinary Pregnancy Heart Team.
  • PK changes: ↑ plasma volume, ↑ CO, ↑ GFR, altered protein binding.
📊 Hypertension

Preferred: Labetalol, Nifedipine, Methyldopa, Metoprolol

Target: BP <140/90 mmHg; severe ≥160/110 → hospital emergency

Emergency: IV labetalol, IV nicardipine, IV urapidil, oral short-acting nifedipine, IV hydralazine (2nd line), IV nitroglycerin for preeclampsia + pulmonary edema

Preeclampsia prevention: Low-dose aspirin 75–150 mg/day from wk 12 to wk 36–37

💔 Heart Failure

Preferred: Metoprolol or bisoprolol; Hydralazine + nitrates; Furosemide if congested; LMWH if EF <35% or intracardiac thrombus.

Acute HF: O₂/ventilation; diuretics if overloaded; nitrates if BP allows; inotropes/vasopressors in shock (dobutamine, milrinone, levosimendan); expert-center care ± mechanical support.

💓 Arrhythmias

SVT: Vagal maneuvers → adenosine → metoprolol for prevention; verapamil in selected cases.

AF/flutter: Rate control: beta-blocker, verapamil, digoxin; unstable → electrical cardioversion.

Avoid routine amiodarone (reserve for life-threatening).

💉 Anticoagulation

Preferred: LMWH — does not cross placenta; strong safety experience; easier outpatient use.

Mechanical heart valves: Very high risk; plan depends on valve type, trimester, thrombosis risk, expert supervision.

Avoid: DOACs.

🚨 Acute Coronary Syndrome
  • Do not delay life-saving care; manage similar to non-pregnant when needed.
  • Preferred antiplatelets: Low-dose aspirin; clopidogrel if DAPT required.
  • Avoid: Ticagrelor; prasugrel only in special situations.
🚫 Drugs to Avoid (Cardiovascular)

ACE inhibitors · ARBs · Sacubitril/valsartan (ARNI) · Aliskiren · Spironolactone · SGLT2 inhibitors · Ivabradine · Atenolol · DOACs · Routine amiodarone · Ticagrelor · Nitroprusside (except last resort).

📊 Monitoring

Maternal: BP log, ECG, echo, BNP/NT-proBNP if HF, renal & liver function, electrolytes, Anti-Xa if therapeutic LMWH.

Fetal: Serial growth US; fetal echo if indicated; FHR during labor in significant heart disease.

🫁 18. Respiratory Diseases in Pregnancy

Core principle: Treat the mother effectively; maternal oxygenation protects the fetus. Untreated respiratory disease is often more dangerous than appropriate therapy.

🌬️ Asthma

Controller: ICS — budesonide preferred; beclomethasone or fluticasone acceptable. Do not stop ICS.

Reliever: SABA (albuterol/salbutamol).

Add-on: LABA (formoterol/salmeterol) only with ICS.

Exacerbation: O₂; nebulized albuterol ± ipratropium; systemic corticosteroids; IV magnesium sulfate for severe refractory attacks.

🤧 Allergic Rhinitis

Preferred: Intranasal corticosteroids (budesonide, beclomethasone, fluticasone); saline irrigation; nasal cromolyn; antihistamines (chlorpheniramine, diphenhydramine, cetirizine, loratadine).

Caution: Oxymetazoline only short course; avoid oral decongestants (esp. pseudoephedrine in 1st tri).

Immunotherapy: Continue if tolerated; do not initiate during pregnancy.

🦠 Influenza

Vaccinate with inactivated or recombinant influenza vaccine.

Treatment: Oseltamivir 75 mg BID × 5 days — start ASAP.

Post-exposure: Oseltamivir 75 mg once daily × 7 days.

Avoid: Live attenuated influenza vaccine.

🫁 Pneumonia

Safer abx: Penicillins (amoxicillin, ampicillin, amox-clav); Cephalosporins (cefuroxime, ceftriaxone, cefpodoxime); Macrolide (azithromycin preferred for atypicals).

Key: Treat early; maintain maternal oxygenation.

🫁 Tuberculosis

Active TB: Isoniazid + Rifampin + Ethambutol; add pyridoxine (B6).

Avoid: Streptomycin.

Caution: Pyrazinamide depends on expert/guideline context.

🌬️ COPD / Chronic Airway Disease
  • Main strategy: smoking cessation; O₂ if hypoxemic; continue needed inhaled bronchodilators; treat exacerbations promptly.
  • Options: SABA (albuterol), SAMA (ipratropium), LABA (salmeterol if specialist-directed), ICS/LABA if asthma overlap, systemic corticosteroids for exacerbations.
✅ Acceptable / Use When Needed

Systemic corticosteroids (prednisone, prednisolone, methylprednisolone) · Montelukast · Cromolyn · Theophylline (rarely; monitor levels) · Omalizumab/selected biologics (severe specialist-managed asthma).

🚫 Avoid or Use with Caution (Respiratory)

Pseudoephedrine in 1st trimester · Ribavirin · Tetracyclines · Fluoroquinolones (unless no safer alternative) · Streptomycin · Long-term topical decongestants · Codeine-containing cough syrups when avoidable · Live attenuated influenza vaccine · Undertreatment of severe asthma.

🫄 19. Gastrointestinal Diseases in Pregnancy

Core principle: Treat the mother safely and effectively. Untreated severe GI disease can be more dangerous to the fetus than properly selected medications.

Goals: Relieve symptoms · maintain nutrition & hydration · control underlying disease · prevent complications · safest effective drug at lowest dose.

🍽️ 19.1 Nausea & Vomiting of Pregnancy

Step 1: Small frequent meals; avoid triggers; hydration; ginger 250 mg QID; Vitamin B6.

Step 2: Pyridoxine (B6) 10–25 mg 3–4×/day ± Doxylamine 12.5–25 mg at bedtime (or combination 10/10 mg QID, titrate).

Step 3: Metoclopramide 10–15 mg PO; Promethazine; Prochlorperazine.

Step 4: Ondansetron 10–15 mg PO (severe cases; avoid high-dose 1st tri unless benefits outweigh risks).

Hyperemesis: IV fluids + thiamine before dextrose + electrolytes + antiemetics.

🔥 19.2 GERD / Heartburn

Step 1: Lifestyle — elevate head, small meals, avoid late meals & triggers.

Step 2: Antacids (CaCO₃, Mg(OH)₂, Al(OH)₃) / Alginates.

Step 3: Famotidine (H2 blocker).

Step 4: PPI (omeprazole/pantoprazole).

🚽 19.3 Constipation

Step 1: Fiber + water + exercise.

Step 2: Bulk laxative (Psyllium, methylcellulose).

Step 3: Docusate (stool softener).

Step 4: PEG or Lactulose (osmotic).

Step 5 (short-term): Senna or Bisacodyl.

💩 19.4 Diarrhea

Step 1: ORS — hydration priority.

Step 2: Loperamide (short-term, if non-invasive).

Step 3: Antibiotics if bacterial infection confirmed/suspected.

Red flags: Fever, bloody stool, severe pain, dehydration, hypotension.

🩸 19.5 Hemorrhoids

Fiber + fluids + stool softeners; sitz baths; topical lidocaine (short-term); avoid prolonged straining; treat constipation aggressively; surgical intervention rarely needed.

🫀 19.6 Gallstones & Biliary Disease

Pregnancy ↑ cholesterol saturation of bile → ↑ gallstones. Symptomatic management: analgesia (acetaminophen; opioids if needed); hydration; if cholangitis/cholecystitis → IV antibiotics + surgical consult. Cholecystectomy can be done in 2nd trimester if indicated.

Avoid: NSAIDs after 20 weeks; high-dose opioid chronic use.

🩹 19.7 Peptic Ulcer Disease

Management: Test & treat H. pylori (when indicated); avoid NSAIDs; stress reduction.

Pharmacologic: Antacids PRN · Sucralfate 1 g QID · Famotidine 10–20 mg BID · Omeprazole 20 mg daily · H. pylori: amoxicillin + clarithromycin (usually deferred till after delivery unless severe).

Avoid: Bismuth subsalicylate (salicylate exposure).

🧬 19.8 Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

Key: Remission is safer than medication withdrawal. Continue effective therapy through pregnancy.

Preferred: 5-ASA (mesalamine, sulfasalazine + folate); Corticosteroids; Thiopurines (azathioprine/6-MP); Anti-TNF (infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab); Vedolizumab; Ustekinumab.

Avoid: Methotrexate; JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib/upadacitinib).

Monitor: Symptoms, CRP, fecal calprotectin, albumin, CBC; fetal growth if severe disease or steroid use.

🫀 19.9 Liver Disease in Pregnancy

Intrahepatic Cholestasis of Pregnancy (ICP): Pruritus (palms/soles); check bile acids & LFTs. Treatment: Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA). Delivery: bile acids >100 μmol/L → 36 wks; <100 → 36–39 wks.

Hepatitis B: Screen all; tenofovir for high viral load; newborn HB vaccine + HBIG.

Hepatitis C: Screen per policy; DAAs deferred until after pregnancy; ribavirin contraindicated.

Hepatitis A/E: Supportive; HEV can be severe → urgent management.

🩺 19.10 Acute Pancreatitis

Causes: Gallstones (most common), hypertriglyceridemia, alcohol, drugs.

Treatment: IV fluids; analgesia (acetaminophen/opioid); antiemetics; nutrition support; treat gallstone disease; insulin/plasmapheresis for severe hypertriglyceridemia; avoid unnecessary antibiotics.

🔪 19.11 Appendicitis & Surgical Conditions

Diagnosis may be delayed (atypical location as uterus grows). Do not delay surgery — appendectomy is safe in all trimesters; laparoscopy preferred in 1st/2nd tri by experienced surgeons.

🚫 GI Drugs to Avoid

Bismuth subsalicylate · Sodium bicarbonate (sodium load) · Misoprostol · Castor oil · Mineral oil (long-term) · Methotrexate · Mycophenolate mofetil · Leflunomide · Tofacitinib/Upadacitinib · NSAIDs after 20 wks · Tetracyclines · Fluoroquinolones · Ribavirin · Aminoglycosides (streptomycin) · High-dose vitamin A.

🚩 Red flags — urgent care

Persistent vomiting · GI bleeding (hematemesis, melena) · severe abdominal pain · jaundice/dark urine/severe pruritus · fever with diarrhea · reduced fetal movements · preterm labor signs.

🧠 20. Pituitary Gland Diseases in Pregnancy

Core principles: Optimize control before conception · use pregnancy-compatible drugs · lowest effective dose · monitor mother & fetus · avoid teratogens · do not stop effective therapy abruptly.

Physiologic changes: ↑ estrogen → ↑ prolactin, ↑ TBG, ↑ CBG · ↑ plasma volume & renal clearance · physiologic hyperprolactinemia · placental transfer of many drugs.

⚪ 20.1 Prolactinoma

Most common pituitary adenoma. Micro (<10 mm) vs Macro (≥10 mm).

Preconception: Cabergoline preferred (higher efficacy, better tolerated); Bromocriptine longest safety experience.

Once pregnant: Usually stop dopamine agonist. Continue only if macroadenoma with risk of growth or visual symptoms.

Risks if untreated: Tumor growth → headache, visual loss; pituitary apoplexy (rare).

📈 20.2 Acromegaly

Excess GH (usually adenoma). Risks: HTN, DM, preeclampsia, fetal growth restriction, macrosomia.

Preconception: Achieve disease control; optimize HTN, DM.

During pregnancy: STOP somatostatin analogs when pregnancy confirmed if disease stable; continue only for significant tumor mass effect.

Options: Octreotide (limited data), Lanreotide (limited), Pegvisomant (specialist only), Dopamine agonists (occasional).

Monitoring: Symptoms; BP, glucose, electrolytes; visual fields each trimester if macroadenoma; glucose tolerance; fetal growth.

⚖️ 20.3 Cushing Syndrome

Preferred: Surgery (transsphenoidal) in 2nd trimester when feasible.

Medical (if surgery not possible): Metyrapone is main option; Ketoconazole specialist-only.

Avoid: Mifepristone, pasireotide, osilodrostat, mitotane.

Monitoring: Cortisol levels; BP, glucose, electrolytes; fetal growth.

💧 20.4 Diabetes Insipidus

Drug of choice: Desmopressin 0.05–0.4 mg PO BID or intranasal. Resistant to placental vasopressinase → effective.

Monitoring: Serum sodium (risk hyponatremia); urine output & osmolality; fluid intake & thirst; signs of overhydration.

Avoid: Excess water intake (hyponatremia); vasopressin (destroyed by placental vasopressinase).

🔬 20.5 Hypopituitarism
  • Glucocorticoid deficiency: Hydrocortisone 15–20 mg/day in 2–3 divided doses; stress dose for labor/illness.
  • Thyroid hormone deficiency: Levothyroxine, adjust dose; monitor TSH & free T4.
  • Gonadotropin deficiency: hCG/FSH for fertility, preconception.
  • GH deficiency: GH not recommended in pregnancy.
  • ADH deficiency: Desmopressin.
✓ Key take-home messages
  • Control disease before conception when possible.
  • Use pregnancy-compatible drugs (Bromocriptine, Cabergoline, Desmopressin, Hydrocortisone, Levothyroxine).
  • Monitor hormone levels & fetal well-being.
  • Plan delivery & postpartum management.
  • Educate patient about emergencies (steroid card).

🦋 21. TSH-Related Diseases in Pregnancy

Overview: TSH is the anterior pituitary hormone regulating thyroid function. In pregnancy, hCG stimulates TSH receptor (mild suppression), estrogen ↑ TBG, iodine demand ↑. Main disorders: hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, subclinical thyroid disease.

🧪 Physiology of TSH in pregnancy
  • hCG can stimulate TSH receptor → mild TSH suppression early.
  • TSH usually lowest in 1st trimester.
  • Total T4 ↑ (TBG rises).
  • Adequate iodine intake essential.
  • Prefer trimester-specific reference ranges.
  • Do not diagnose on TSH alone — correlate with free T4, symptoms, antibodies, clinical context.
🔽 21.1 Hypothyroidism

Causes: Hashimoto's thyroiditis; prior thyroidectomy/radioiodine; iodine deficiency; overtreatment of hyperthyroidism.

Features: High TSH, low free T4; fatigue, constipation, cold intolerance, weight gain, bradycardia.

Risks: Miscarriage, anemia, preeclampsia, placental abruption, impaired fetal neurodevelopment.

Treatment: Levothyroxine (LT4). Increase dose early once pregnancy confirmed. Take on empty stomach; separate from iron/calcium by 4 hrs. Monitor TSH every 4 wks during adjustment.

Target: TSH <2.5 mIU/L in 1st trimester and generally <3 mIU/L later.

Pearls: Never use liothyronine (T3) as routine replacement in pregnancy.

⚖️ 21.2 Subclinical Hypothyroidism

TSH elevated with normal free T4. Consider treatment if TSH clearly elevated or TPO antibodies positive. Levothyroxine preferred. Monitor closely — pregnancy ↑ thyroid hormone requirement.

🔼 21.3 Hyperthyroidism / Thyrotoxicosis

Causes: Graves' disease (most important); gestational transient thyrotoxicosis.

Features: Low/suppressed TSH; ↑ free T4 or T3; weight loss, heat intolerance, tachycardia, anxiety.

Risks: Maternal AF, HF, preeclampsia, fetal loss, growth restriction, preterm birth.

Management:

  • 1st trimester: Propylthiouracil (PTU).
  • 2nd–3rd: Methimazole usually preferred (PTU hepatotoxicity).
  • Use lowest effective dose; avoid overtreatment (fetal hypothyroidism).
  • Propranolol short-term for severe adrenergic symptoms.
  • Gestational transient thyrotoxicosis often needs supportive care only.
🚨 21.4 Thyroid Storm

Emergency: PTU + beta-blocker (propranolol) + iodine (after thionamide) + corticosteroids + supportive care + ICU monitoring.

🧠 21.5 Central Thyroid Disease

TSH may be low, normal, or unreliable. Follow free T4 rather than TSH alone. Treat underlying cause; coordinate with endocrinology.

🚫 Avoid / Not Routine

Radioactive iodine (contraindicated) · excess iodine exposure · long-term high-dose beta-blockers · T3-only therapy.

✓ Key take-home messages
  • TSH is key screening/monitoring marker — interpret with free T4 & clinical context.
  • Levothyroxine for hypothyroidism.
  • PTU preferred in 1st tri; methimazole usually preferred later.
  • Radioactive iodine contraindicated.
  • Tight monitoring improves maternal & fetal outcomes.

🍬 22. Glucose Metabolism Diseases in Pregnancy

Core principle: Poor glycemic control causes miscarriage, congenital malformations, macrosomia, preeclampsia, preterm birth, neonatal hypoglycemia, stillbirth. Insulin is preferred first-line when medication needed.

🟢 Category 1 — Preferred / Generally Safe

Insulin: Rapid-acting (lispro, aspart); short-acting (regular); basal (NPH, detemir, glargine). Does not cross placenta.

Dextrose: For hypoglycemia emergencies.

Low-dose aspirin: When indicated for preeclampsia risk reduction.

🟡 Category 2 — Acceptable / Use When Needed

Metformin: May be used in selected GDM or T2DM when benefits outweigh risks; crosses placenta.

Glyburide/glibenclamide: Less preferred; may cause more neonatal hypoglycemia/macrosomia than insulin.

Glucagon: Emergency for severe hypoglycemia.

🔴 Category 3 — Avoid / Contraindicated

SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin) · GLP-1 RA (semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide) · DPP-4 inhibitors (sitagliptin, linagliptin) · Sulfonylureas as routine first-line · ACE inhibitors/ARBs · Routine statins · Weight-loss drugs.

📈 Stepwise management of GDM
  1. Screen/diagnose
  2. Lifestyle: medical nutrition therapy + exercise (if safe)
  3. Glucose monitoring (SMBG/CGM)
  4. If fasting or post-meal targets not met → start insulin
  5. Titrate per SMBG
  6. Fetal surveillance if medication-treated or poorly controlled
Common targets: Fasting <95 mg/dL · 1-hr post-meal <140 mg/dL · 2-hr post-meal <120 mg/dL.
💉 Insulin strategy
  • Basal controls fasting; rapid/short-acting controls meals.
  • Requirements often ↑ later in pregnancy; may fall rapidly postpartum.
  • Teach injection technique, hypoglycemia recognition, meal timing.
🚨 DKA in pregnancy

Can occur at lower glucose levels than outside pregnancy. Triggers: infection, vomiting, missed insulin, steroids, pump failure.

Treatment: IV fluids + IV insulin + potassium monitoring/replacement + treat trigger + fetal & maternal monitoring. Do not delay.

⚠️ Hypoglycemia

Mild: fast oral glucose (15 g carbs). Severe: glucagon or IV dextrose. Recheck & adjust regimen.

✓ Key take-home messages
  • Insulin is the preferred pharmacologic therapy.
  • Metformin acceptable in selected cases but not first-line everywhere.
  • Avoid SGLT2i, GLP-1 RA, DPP-4i, ACEi, ARBs, routine statins.
  • Treat DKA urgently.
  • Good maternal glucose control is the best fetal protection.

🦴 23. Calcium Metabolism Diseases in Pregnancy

Physiology: ↑ GI calcium absorption (1,25(OH)₂D); ↑ renal calcium retention; maternal bone resorption for fetal needs; placenta actively transports calcium (esp. 3rd tri); changes in PTH, vitamin D, calcitonin.

Goals: Prevent hypo/hypercalcemia; normal skeletal & neuro development; prevent nephrolithiasis, bone loss, fractures; prepare for delivery & lactation.

📈 23.1 Hypercalcemia (Primary Hyperparathyroidism)

Nausea, vomiting, constipation; polyuria, polydipsia, nephrolithiasis; bone pain, muscle weakness.

Risks: Preterm birth, low birth weight, preeclampsia, miscarriage.

Treatment:

  • Avoid thiazides & lithium.
  • Definitive: Parathyroidectomy (2nd trimester preferred).
  • Medical: IV isotonic saline; calcitonin (does not cross placenta significantly); cinacalcet (when surgery not possible); bisphosphonates (limited data).
📉 23.2 Hypocalcemia (Hypoparathyroidism, Vit D Deficiency)

Perioral numbness, cramps, tetany; seizures, laryngospasm (severe).

Risks: Preterm birth, fetal tetany, neonatal hypocalcemia.

Treatment: Calcium carbonate/citrate (safe); Vitamin D3 (safe); Calcitriol (safe, lowest dose); Magnesium (if low, safe).

Avoid: Aluminum-containing antacids; high-dose vitamin D without monitoring.

☀️ 23.3 Vitamin D Deficiency

Bone pain, muscle weakness. Risks: GDM, preeclampsia, preterm birth, low birth weight.

Treatment: Monitor 25(OH)D. Vitamin D3 600–2,000 IU/day; deficiency up to 4,000 IU/day under supervision; 50,000 IU weekly for deficiency; Calcitriol for severe cases.

🦴 23.4 Pregnancy-Related Osteoporosis

Severe back pain, vertebral fractures; usually in 3rd trimester or postpartum. Treatment: calcium, pain control; limited data for antiresorptives.

🚫 Avoid

Aluminum-containing antacids · high-dose vitamin D without monitoring · thiazide diuretics (hypercalcemia risk) · lithium (hypercalcemia risk).

💎 24. Adrenal Gland Diseases in Pregnancy

Core principles: Stabilize mother to protect fetus · pregnancy-compatible drugs at lowest effective dose · avoid undertreatment · multidisciplinary care (Endo, OB, Anesthesia, Neonatology).

Features: Fatigue, nausea/vomiting, weight loss, postural hypotension, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia.

Treatment:

  • Hydrocortisone first-line.
  • Fludrocortisone for mineralocorticoid replacement.
  • Increase hydrocortisone by 20–40% later in pregnancy.
  • Stress-dose steroids for illness, surgery, labor, delivery.

⚖️ 24.3 Cushing Syndrome

Surgery in 2nd trimester preferred. Metyrapone main medical option. Ketoconazole specialist-only. Avoid mifepristone.

🩸 24.4 Primary Aldosteronism

HTN + hypokalemia. Use labetalol/nifedipine/methyldopa; KCl supplementation; eplerenone if needed; avoid spironolactone if possible.

Avoid: ACE inhibitors/ARBs (fetal renal toxicity).

🔺 24.5 Pheochromocytoma

Severe HTN, headache, palpitations, sweating — life-threatening if missed.

Treatment:

  • Alpha-blockade first: Phenoxybenzamine, Doxazosin.
  • Beta-blocker only after adequate alpha-blockade if tachycardia persists (propranolol/metoprolol).
  • Surgery in 2nd trimester if diagnosed early; otherwise medical control until delivery.
🚫 Never give beta-blocker before alpha-blockade — risk of unopposed alpha stimulation → hypertensive crisis.
🧬 24.6 CAH / Steroid-Dependent Adrenal Disorders

Continue essential steroid replacement; hydrocortisone preferred; fludrocortisone if salt-wasting; stress-dose steroids in labor & severe illness.

✓ Key take-home messages

Better recognition + safe pharmacology + close monitoring = better outcomes for mother & baby.

📊 26. Growth Hormone Related Diseases

GH physiology in pregnancy: ↑ hGH secretion (2–3×); ↑ placental GH & GHBP; ↓ GH receptor sensitivity; ↑ IGF-1 (mainly placental). Result: physiologic insulin resistance, lipolysis, fetal growth support.

📈 26.1 Acromegaly

Pregnancy generally well tolerated when disease is controlled. Active disease or tumor growth risk requires careful management.

  • Before conception: Achieve control with surgery, somatostatin analogs ± pegvisomant; normalize IGF-1; control tumor size.
  • During pregnancy: STOP somatostatin analogs when pregnancy confirmed if disease controlled; continue only if tumor mass effect or symptoms recur.
  • Pegvisomant: not recommended.
  • Surgery (transsphenoidal) if significant tumor growth — safe in 2nd trimester.

Monitoring: Clinical assessment each trimester; visual fields if adenoma; IGF-1 not reliable; MRI without contrast if needed.

📏 26.2 Gigantism

Same approach as acromegaly; ensure control before conception; long-term metabolic follow-up.

🔽 26.3 GHD

GH not essential for pregnancy survival. Stop GH when pregnancy confirmed. No evidence of teratogenicity but not indicated. No need for IGF-1 monitoring.

⚪ 26.4 Prolactinoma & Hyperprolactinemia

Prolactin rises physiologically in pregnancy. Risk of tumor enlargement in macroadenomas.

  • Microprolactinoma: stop dopamine agonist once pregnant; monitor clinically.
  • Macroadenoma: stop but monitor clinically.
  • Symptom/visual changes: restart dopamine agonist (bromocriptine preferred; cabergoline if benefits outweigh risks).
  • Prolactin levels NOT useful for monitoring in pregnancy.

⚡ 27. ACTH Hormone-Related Diseases

ACTH physiology: Placental CRH & ACTH contribute to rising maternal cortisol. CBG ↑ → total cortisol ↑. Free cortisol also ↑ later. Interpretation of ACTH–cortisol testing must consider pregnancy physiology.

🔽 27.1 Secondary Adrenal Insufficiency / ACTH Deficiency

Causes: Hypopituitarism, Sheehan syndrome, pituitary surgery, apoplexy.

Symptoms: Fatigue, nausea, hypotension, dizziness, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia.

Drug of choice: Hydrocortisone 15–25 mg/day divided doses. Alternatives: prednisolone, prednisone.

Notes:

  • Usually NO fludrocortisone needed in isolated ACTH deficiency.
  • Avoid routine dexamethasone for replacement (crosses placenta more).
  • 20–40% glucocorticoid dose increase in 2nd–3rd trimester.
  • Stress dosing essential for labor, surgery, vomiting illness, infection, trauma.
🚨 27.2 Adrenal Crisis

Red flags: Severe vomiting, collapse, shock, confusion, profound weakness, dehydration, hypoglycemia.

Immediate: Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV/IM immediately → 200 mg over 24 h or 50 mg IV/IM q6h. IV isotonic saline; dextrose if hypoglycemic. Treat precipitating cause. Do NOT delay.

⚖️ 27.3 ACTH-Dependent Cushing Syndrome

Includes pituitary Cushing disease and ectopic ACTH syndrome.

Risks: Severe HTN, DM, preeclampsia, infections, hypokalemia, fetal growth restriction, prematurity.

Management:

  • Preferred: surgery in 2nd trimester.
  • If surgery not possible: Metyrapone (leading medical option); monitor for worsening HTN & hypokalemia.
  • Cabergoline may be considered in selected pituitary Cushing disease cases.
  • Ketoconazole: limited data, specialist rescue only.
  • Ectopic ACTH: stabilize hypercortisolism first, then localize/treat source with expert care.

Avoid: Mifepristone, pasireotide, osilodrostat.

🩺 Labor & delivery steroid plan
  • Continue usual morning replacement dose.
  • Stress-dose hydrocortisone: 100 mg IV at onset of active labor/anesthesia, then 50 mg q6h or continuous infusion.
  • Taper back to baseline within 24–48 hrs if stable.
✓ Key take-home messages
  • Hydrocortisone is first-choice replacement in pregnancy.
  • Isolated ACTH deficiency: fludrocortisone usually not needed.
  • Increase glucocorticoid later in pregnancy when clinically required.
  • Adrenal crisis = emergency → treat immediately.
  • Cushing: 2nd-trimester surgery preferred; metyrapone is main medical option.
  • Avoid mifepristone & routine dexamethasone replacement.

👶 28. Oxytocin-Related Diseases

Physiology: Estrogen ↑ oxytocin receptors in late pregnancy. Positive feedback in labor: cervical stretch → oxytocin release → stronger contractions → more stretch. Facilitates milk ejection by acting on myoepithelial cells. Synergizes with prostaglandins.

👶 28.1 Labor Induction

Indications: Post-term, PROM, maternal medical conditions (HTN, DM), fetal demise.

Oxytocin dosing (IV):

  • Start 0.5–2 mIU/min
  • Increase 1–2 every 20–40 min
  • Max 20–40 (do not exceed without review)

Monitor: Contraction frequency/duration/intensity; FHR continuous; maternal vitals.

Contraindications: Cephalopelvic disproportion; non-reassuring fetal status; previous uterine rupture; placenta previa (major); severe fetal distress.

📈 28.2 Labor Augmentation

Begin when contractions inadequate (<3 in 10 min). Target 3–5 contractions in 10 min, each 40–60 sec.

Risks: Tachysystole (>5 in 10 min), fetal distress, uterine hypertonicity, uterine rupture (rare).

Management of tachysystole: Stop oxytocin · left lateral · IV fluids · O₂ by mask · consider tocolytic (terbutaline).

🩸 28.3 Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH)

Uterine atony is most common cause. Oxytocin is uterotonic of choice.

Uterine hyperstimulation/tachysystole · fetal distress · hypotension · nausea/vomiting · water intoxication (hyponatremia).

🥚 29. LH & FSH Related Diseases

Physiology in pregnancy: Ovulation stops; new follicle recruitment is suppressed; corpus luteum is rescued by hCG in early pregnancy; E + P maintain suppression of pituitary gonadotropins. Active gonadotropin stimulation is generally NOT continued once pregnancy is confirmed.

⚖️ 29.1 PCOS & Ovulatory Dysfunction

Before conception: Letrozole first-line; Clomiphene alternative; Metformin if insulin resistance.

During pregnancy: Stop ovulation-induction drugs; continue only meds clearly indicated. Weight optimization, glucose monitoring, BP monitoring, early GDM screening.

Risks: Miscarriage, GDM, hypertensive disorders.

🔽 29.2 Hypogonadotropic Hypogonadism

Low LH/FSH, absent ovulation, infertility. Preconception: pulsatile GnRH or gonadotropins (FSH ± LH, hMG). Once pregnant: discontinue ovulation-induction therapy.

🕐 29.3 Premature Ovarian Insufficiency

High FSH, low estrogen, infertility. Fertility treatment often requires assisted reproduction. HRT is preconception care, not routine pregnancy therapy.

⚡ 29.4 Ovarian Hyperstimulation Syndrome (OHSS) Risk

Triggered by exogenous FSH/hMG ± hCG. Warning signs: abdominal pain, ovarian enlargement, ascites, hemoconcentration.

Management: Stop stimulation cycle; fluid balance; thrombosis risk assessment; urgent specialist review if severe. Pregnancy can worsen OHSS.

✓ Key take-home messages
  • LH/FSH are essential for ovulation but physiologically suppressed in pregnancy.
  • Most LH/FSH-directed drugs are fertility treatments, not continued in pregnancy.
  • PCOS is the most common clinical reason.
  • Letrozole is first-line ovulation induction in PCOS; clomiphene is alternative.
  • Once pregnant: stop ovulation-induction drugs.
  • Metformin, insulin, progesterone may continue if clinically indicated.

💧 30. Prolactin-Related Diseases

Physiology: Estrogen stimulates lactotroph hyperplasia & ↑ prolactin. High progesterone during pregnancy limits full milk secretion until after delivery. After birth: ↓ progesterone + suckling → milk let-down & production.

Clinical pearl: Serum prolactin rises normally in pregnancy → routine prolactin measurement NOT useful for tumor monitoring.

⚪ 30.1 Microprolactinoma

Stop dopamine agonist once pregnancy confirmed. Do not use prolactin levels alone to guide management. Multidisciplinary care essential.

🚨 30.2 Symptomatic Tumor Enlargement
  • Warning: progressive headache, visual field defect, cranial nerve symptoms.
  • Urgent MRI without gadolinium.
  • Visual field testing.
  • Restart bromocriptine.
  • Surgery only if refractory/severe compression.
🍼 30.3 Poor Lactation / Lactation Failure

Consider pituitary insufficiency, Sheehan syndrome, severe postpartum hemorrhage, retained placental hormone effects, or inadequate breastfeeding technique. Management: correct cause, lactation support, hydration, endocrinology review.

💊 30.4 Galactorrhea / Drug-Induced Hyperprolactinemia

Common culprits: antipsychotics, metoclopramide, domperidone, some antidepressants. Review medication necessity; do not stop psychiatric drugs abruptly. Treat underlying cause.

✓ Key take-home messages
  • Physiologic prolactin rise in pregnancy is normal.
  • Microprolactinoma: stop dopamine agonist once pregnant.
  • Macroprolactinoma or symptomatic growth: bromocriptine is usual first drug.
  • Do not use prolactin levels alone to guide management.
  • Multidisciplinary care essential.

💦 31. ADH (Vasopressin) Related Diseases

Physiology: Estrogen stimulates ADH release; ↑ plasma volume & ↓ plasma osmolality; ↑ reset osmostat (ADH released at lower osmolality); ↑ V2 receptor sensitivity. Result: tendency to water retention & dilutional hyponatremia, esp. late pregnancy.

💧 31.1 SIADH

Causes: Preeclampsia/eclampsia; pulmonary disorders; CNS lesions; medications (SSRIs, carbamazepine); nausea, pain, stress.

Features: ↓ Serum Na⁺; low serum osmolality; inappropriately concentrated urine; euvolemic.

Management: Fluid restriction; salt tablets (NaCl 1–3 g/day); vasopressin antagonists (tolvaptan — avoid in pregnancy unless benefit > risk); monitor Na⁺ q4–6h until stable.

Avoid: Vaptans in 1st trimester; excessive IV fluids.

💦 31.2 Diabetes Insipidus

Types: Central DI (↓ ADH); nephrogenic DI (↓ renal response); gestational DI (rare, transient).

Features: Polyuria (>3 L/day), polydipsia; dilute urine (↓ osmolality); hypernatremia if inadequate water intake.

Preferred: Desmopressin (DDAVP) — drug of choice for central DI. Oral/nasal/SC/IV. Start low, titrate to symptoms & Na⁺.

Monitoring: Serum Na⁺, urine osmolality, fluid balance.

Notes: Avoid overcorrection → hyponatremia. Nephrogenic DI: treat cause; thiazides ± NSAIDs with caution. Ensure adequate water intake.

🌊 31.3 Dilutional Hyponatremia

Physiologic in late pregnancy (mild, Na⁺ 130–135 mEq/L).

  • Mild: reassurance; limit excessive fluid intake.
  • Moderate/severe (<130): fluid restriction + treat underlying cause.
  • Severe with symptoms: 3% hypertonic saline with caution.
  • Monitor Na⁺ q6–12h; avoid rapid correction (>8–10 mEq/L/24h).
💊 31.4 Polyuria / Water Imbalance in Obstetric Conditions

Associated with preeclampsia, acute fatty liver of pregnancy, hyperemesis gravidarum. Management: treat underlying; monitor fluid balance; correct electrolytes.

🚫 Avoid

Vasopressin (high dose) · hypotonic IV fluids · excessive desmopressin · diuretics unless indicated · carbamazepine/phenytoin (SIADH-like effect).

🍬 32. Pancreas-Related Diseases

Physiology: Insulin resistance from placental hormones (hPL, progesterone, cortisol); ↑ β-cell workload; ↑ risk of hyperglycemia & pancreatitis. Exocrine secretion maintained.

🍬 32.1 Gestational Diabetes (GDM)

Glucose intolerance first recognized in pregnancy. ↑ macrosomia, preeclampsia, C-section, neonatal hypoglycemia.

💉 32.2 Pre-existing Diabetes (T1 or T2)

Higher risk: congenital anomalies, miscarriage, preeclampsia, stillbirth. Preconception control crucial.

  • Glucose monitoring 7×/day; A1c each trimester.

🩺 32.3 Acute Pancreatitis

Causes: Gallstones (most common), hypertriglyceridemia, meds, idiopathic. 2nd/3rd tri most common.

Management: Hospitalize; aggressive IV fluids; bowel rest; pain control (acetaminophen/opioids); treat underlying; early enteral nutrition when tolerated; antibiotics only if infection (cholangitis, necrosis).

🧪 32.4 Exocrine Pancreatic Insufficiency (EPI)

Causes: chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, fibrosis. Leads to malabsorption, weight loss, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency.

  • Pancreatic enzyme replacement — first-line; considered safe; not systemically absorbed.
  • Vitamins (A, D, E, K) — supplement as needed.
  • PPIs (omeprazole/pantoprazole) to reduce acid inactivation of enzymes.
🚫 Avoid (Diabetes/Pancreas)

Sulfonylureas (glyburide, glipizide) as routine · DPP-4 inhibitors · GLP-1 RA · ACE inhibitors · ARBs.

✓ Key take-home messages
  • Insulin is the gold standard for diabetes in pregnancy.
  • Metformin acceptable in selected cases.
  • Pancreatic enzyme replacement is safe in EPI.
  • Treat acute pancreatitis with fluids, analgesia, nutrition, cause control.

🫘 34. Urinary System Related Diseases

Overview: Pregnancy ↑ urinary stasis (progesterone relaxes smooth muscle; enlarging uterus compresses tract). Common: asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB), acute cystitis, pyelonephritis, recurrent UTI. Untreated → pyelonephritis, sepsis, anemia, ARDS, preterm birth, LBW.

Screening: Once early in prenatal care with urine culture. ASB = ≥10⁵ CFU/mL without symptoms.

🔹 34.1 Asymptomatic Bacteriuria (ASB)

Treat all pregnant patients with ASB. Course: 4–7 days. Options based on culture: nitrofurantoin, cephalexin, amoxicillin if only susceptible. Follow-up culture 1–2 wks after treatment.

🔹 34.2 Acute Cystitis

Treat 5–7 days. Preferred: nitrofurantoin, cephalexin, fosfomycin single dose for lower UTI. Avoid empiric amoxicillin/ampicillin (high resistance). Reassess if symptoms persist after 48 hrs.

🔹 34.3 Pyelonephritis

Fever, flank pain, CVA tenderness, N/V → urgent treatment.

  • Inpatient; IV fluids + IV antibiotics with gram-negative coverage.
  • Common: ceftriaxone or cefuroxime; step down to oral after improvement.
  • Total 14 days.
  • Nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin NOT appropriate for pyelonephritis.
🔹 34.4 Recurrent UTI

Suppressive: nitrofurantoin 100 mg nightly or cephalexin 250–500 mg nightly. Culture-guided follow-up.

🦠 35. Bacterial Infections in Pregnancy

Why pregnancy ↑ risk: Urinary stasis & ureteral dilation → higher UTI risk; altered immunity; vaginal microbiome changes → bacterial vaginosis; untreated → pyelonephritis, PPROM, preterm labor, neonatal infection, maternal sepsis.

🩸 35.1 GBS Colonization

Screen at 36–37+6 wks. Intrapartum prophylaxis if GBS positive, GBS bacteriuria, or prior infant with invasive GBS.

Preferred: Penicillin G IV 5 million units loading → 2.5–3 million units IV q4h until delivery.

Alternative: Ampicillin IV; if severe PCN allergy → susceptibility-guided alternative.

🤰 35.2 Chorioamnionitis

Maternal fever, uterine tenderness, fetal tachycardia, foul fluid.

Treatment: Ampicillin + gentamicin intrapartum; add clindamycin at cesarean delivery. Reduces maternal & neonatal complications.

💊 35.3 Chlamydia / Gonorrhea

Chlamydia: Azithromycin 1 g PO once. Test of cure later in pregnancy; treat partner(s).

Gonorrhea: Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM once. Add chlamydia treatment if not excluded. Treat partner(s).

💊 35.4 Bacterial Vaginosis

Metronidazole 500 mg PO BID × 7 days OR Clindamycin 300 mg PO BID × 7 days. Avoid tinidazole.

🥛 35.5 Listeriosis

Fever, flu-like illness, fetal risk, contaminated food exposure.

Treatment: Ampicillin IV or high-dose; gentamicin may be added in severe cases.

Prevention: Avoid unpasteurized dairy & high-risk refrigerated foods.

🌡️ 35.6 Syphilis

Penicillin is the only proven therapy to prevent congenital syphilis.

Treatment: Benzathine penicillin G according to stage.

Penicillin allergy: Desensitize, then treat with penicillin.

✅ Preferred antibiotics

Penicillins · Cephalosporins · Fosfomycin · Azithromycin · Clindamycin · Metronidazole

🚫 Avoid

Tetracyclines · Fluoroquinolones · Chloramphenicol near term · Tinidazole in pregnancy

🤕 42. Migraine in Pregnancy

Overview: Migraine often improves during pregnancy (esp. 2nd/3rd tri) due to stable estrogen, but can persist or worsen. Treat promptly; rule out secondary causes (preeclampsia, stroke, venous sinus thrombosis).

🛠️ Non-drug first steps
  • Identify & avoid triggers (sleep, diet, stress, hydration).
  • Dark, quiet room; cold compress; rest.
  • Regular sleep & meals; hydration; moderate caffeine.
  • Relaxation, biofeedback, acupuncture, CBT.
💊 Acute treatment (preferred / commonly used)
  • First-line: Acetaminophen (max 3–4 g/day).
  • Add caffeine (low dose) as adjunct in some formulations.
  • Refractory/severe: Triptans — sumatriptan preferred (most pregnancy data); use if benefits outweigh risks.
  • Antiemetics: metoclopramide, prochlorperazine; ondansetron if refractory.
  • Opioids: reserve for refractory severe pain; avoid chronic use.
🟡 NSAIDs — trimester guidance
  • 1st tri: avoid if possible (miscarriage signal).
  • 2nd tri: acceptable short-term.
  • 3rd tri: avoid (ductus arteriosus closure, oligohydramnios, labor interference).
🛡️ Preventive therapy for frequent migraine

Consider if attacks are frequent, disabling, or prolonged.

  • Beta-blockers: propranolol, metoprolol (preferred).
  • Topiramate: avoid (oral cleft risk).
  • Valproate: avoid (neural tube defects, cognitive impairment).
  • Amitriptyline/nortriptyline: options if indicated.
  • Magnesium: may be considered (consult specialist).
🚫 Drugs to Avoid or Generally Not Preferred

Ergotamines (ergotamine, dihydroergotamine) · Valproate · Topiramate · High-dose or chronic NSAIDs · Opioid chronic use · Combination analgesics with high caffeine or butalbital.

✓ Key take-home messages

Acetaminophen first-line · sumatriptan preferred triptan if needed · avoid NSAIDs in 3rd tri · propranolol/metoprolol preferred preventives · avoid valproate & topiramate · individualize therapy & monitor.

🛡️ 45. Autoimmune Diseases in Pregnancy

Overview: Affect women of childbearing age more commonly. Pregnancy can influence disease activity; active disease ↑ adverse maternal & fetal outcomes. Goal: achieve remission/low activity before conception; use safest effective meds; monitor closely.

Principles: Plan pregnancy when stable (≥6 months if possible) · continue essential meds · lowest effective dose · prefer drugs with established safety · avoid teratogens · coordinate rheumatology, OB, MFM, pharmacist.

✓ Key take-home messages

🧠 46. Psychiatric Disorders in Pregnancy

  • Congenital malformation risk depends on drug & timing.
  • Preterm birth/LBW may relate to illness severity & some medication exposure.
  • Neonatal adaptation syndrome (withdrawal, sedation).

💊 47. Antiepileptics, Antidepressants, Anxiolytics & Antipsychotics

General principles: Treat maternal illness effectively — uncontrolled conditions harm mother & fetus. Lowest effective dose of a single agent when possible. Avoid polytherapy. Prefer drugs with more safety data. Preconception counseling. Folic acid 0.4–4 mg/day. Monitor drug levels when available.

🧪 48. Conducting Clinical Trials in Pregnant Women

🤔 Why include pregnant women?
  • Pregnancy alters PK/PD → affects efficacy & safety.
  • Exclusion → therapeutic uncertainty.
  • Ethical obligation to generate data.
  • Regulators now encourage careful inclusion.
⚖️ Ethical principles
  • Respect for persons: Voluntary participation; autonomy.
  • Beneficence: Maximize benefit for mother/fetus.
  • Nonmaleficence: Minimize risk via design & monitoring.
  • Justice: Avoid unfair exclusion; ensure equitable access.
🧠 Key design considerations
  • Rigorous risk–benefit & scientific justification.
  • Preclinical reproductive toxicity data required.
  • Pregnancy-specific dosing & monitoring plans.
  • Independent Data Safety Monitoring Board.
  • Adaptive designs & stopping rules.
  • Lactation considerations when relevant.
📋 Regulatory framework
  • FDA: Encourages inclusion when appropriate; no categorical exclusion.
  • ICH: E8(R1) General Considerations; E11(R1) Pediatric Trials.
  • CIOMS: Ethical Considerations for Research in Pregnancy.
📊 Pregnancy exposure registries

What: Observational studies following pregnant individuals exposed to medications (therapeutic or accidental).

Purpose: Detect rare/long-term outcomes; complement trials & postmarketing data; inform labeling & guidelines.

Types:

  • Prospective: Enroll during pregnancy & follow outcomes — e.g., OTIS, MotherToBaby.
  • Retrospective: Use existing data — e.g., Medicaid databases, claims data.
  • Condition-specific: Antiepileptic Pregnancy Registry, SSRIs in Pregnancy Registry.

Best practices: Standardized collection & definitions; large diverse populations; active follow-up; data privacy; transparent reporting.

🤝 Informed consent & shared decision-making
  • Consider cultural, spiritual, social factors.
  • Address potential postpartum mental health implications.
  • Include partner/family when appropriate & desired.
  • Revisit decisions as pregnancy progresses or new data emerge.
✓ Key take-home messages
  • Pregnant individuals should not be routinely excluded from research.
  • Well-designed trials & registries are critical to reduce therapeutic uncertainty.
  • Informed consent & shared decision-making empower patients & improve outcomes.
  • Ethical & regulatory frameworks aim to protect while enabling progress.
